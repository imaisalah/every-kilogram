# Every Kilogram: teacher sheet (one 45-minute lesson)

**Ages:** 13–18 · **Group size:** pairs, one laptop or tablet per pair · **Needs:** a web browser; no install, no accounts.
**Big question:** *How do engineers turn a science question into a spacecraft that works, on time and on budget?*

In **Classroom mode** each pair runs a young space agency through one real mission: **find ice buried under the dust of Mars**. They write a proposal, design an orbiter part by part, defend it to review boards, react to events taken from real NASA history, launch through a real Mars window, and compare their spacecraft with NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO). Every number in the game links to its NASA source.

**How to open it:** on the title screen press **Classroom mode**, or share the game address with `#classroom` at the end (for example `https://…/every-kilogram/#classroom`). A slim coach bar stays at the top of every screen and tells students what to do, why it matters and which science practice they are using. Six checkpoints ask short questions; the answers build a **mission log** that students print or download at the end.

**Teacher view:** the switch in the coach bar (and on the briefing page) shows the learning objectives, the NGSS mapping, a teaching tip for every step and answer hints for every checkpoint. Turn it off before handing a laptop back.

**What the class starts with:** January 2029, reputation 2 (the Mars contract is open on day one), $650M, two engineering teams, and three research items already finished (3 m dish, 100 W radio, aerobraking). From January 2029 a typical Mars design is ready in late 2030, just in time for the January–April 2031 window. A slip of a few months can still cost that window, which is the real lesson of InSight.

---

## 1. Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson students can:

1. **Turn a science goal into requirements** they can measure: mass, power, data, cost and time.
2. **Run a trade study** and justify a design with evidence, not preference ("the 3 m dish adds mass but doubles the data rate").
3. **Explain the mass snowball:** every kilogram needs propellant, which is more mass (the rocket equation).
4. **Explain why sunlight weakens with distance** (1/r²): 1,361 W/m² at Earth, 586 W/m² at Mars, so the panels must be bigger.
5. **Explain why Mars launch windows come about every 26 months**, and what missing one costs.
6. **Manage a budget with a reserve**, and explain why NASA holds back about 25% for surprises.
7. **Connect a real NASA failure to the process fix** that prevents it, and **defend a decision** to a review board with data.

Science and Engineering Practices named in the coach bar: asking questions and defining problems; developing and using models; analyzing and interpreting data; using mathematics and computational thinking; constructing explanations and designing solutions; engaging in argument from evidence; obtaining, evaluating and communicating information.
Crosscutting concepts: systems and system models; scale, proportion and quantity; energy and matter; influence of engineering on society.

**If you are short of time:** skip step 6 by pressing **Skip to next event** twice, and run the debrief as a whole-class discussion.
**Extension (a second lesson or homework):** play a normal career from the title screen. Mars unlocks at reputation 2; Venus and Europa need more reputation and research.

## 5. Discussion questions

1. **Mass:** Which part did you remove or shrink to save mass? What did you lose by doing it, and was it worth it?
2. **Margins:** Your design met every requirement exactly. Why do real engineers still add 20–30% extra power or mass margin?
3. **Reserves:** Lean proposals win more reputation but carry a small reserve. When would you choose Lean, and when Safe? Use what happened to your project as evidence.
4. **Testing:** Mars Polar Lander's test was run with mis-wired sensors and never repeated. Hubble's mirror passed one test while other tests showed a problem. What rule would you write for your test team?
5. **Distance:** Sunlight at Jupiter is about 27 times weaker than at Earth. How would your Mars design have to change to work at Jupiter? (Hint: Europa Clipper's two solar arrays are each 14.2 m long and make only about 700 W.)
6. **Society:** Who would use a map of buried ice on Mars, and for what decisions? (Landing-site choice for crews, in-situ fuel production.)

**Exit ticket (one sentence each, written in the mission log):** *One trade-off we made was ___ because ___.* · *One real NASA lesson we will remember is ___.*

### Answer hints (also shown in Teacher view)

- **Proposal:** a good answer ties the reserve to risk. Lean (10%) turns one surprise into an overrun; past 15% cost growth Congress is notified. Standard (25%) is NASA's Discovery rule. Safe (40%) costs more up front.
- **Design:** both sides of a trade-off with numbers. Without the shallow radar sounder the headline goal (buried ice) cannot score.
- **Review board:** quotes the board's number ("power margin 8%, engineers want 20%") and weighs fixing now against risk later.
- **Real NASA story:** links the story to a process fix: end-to-end tests, a second team cross-checking, margin, re-verifying heritage parts.
- **Launch window:** Earth and Mars line up for a low-energy transfer about every 26 months; the trip is half an ellipse around the Sun, about 7–9 months; a missed window means a two-year wait and storage costs.
- **Results:** quotes one "What shaped this mission" line with its number and compares fairly with the prediction.

## 6. Real stories you can tell (each can appear as an in-game event card)

- **Mars Climate Orbiter (1999):** one team used pound-force seconds, another newton-seconds. The orbiter flew too low at Mars and was lost. *Lesson: test interfaces end to end.*
- **InSight (2015):** a small leak could not be fixed before the 2016 Mars window. The next window was 26 months later and the delay cost about $153.8M. *Lesson: planetary windows are hard deadlines.*
- **Galileo (1991):** the big antenna never opened. Compression and ground antennas rescued the science. *Lesson: deployables are single points of failure.*
- **IMAP (2025):** launched 3 months early and returned $22.2M of unused reserve. *Lesson: good management is rewarded.*

## 8. Assessment rubric

| Criterion | 4 — Excellent | 3 — Proficient | 2 — Developing | 1 — Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Requirements and constraints** (MS-ETS1-1, HS-ETS1-1) | Names every requirement and the limit that bound the design, with numbers | Names most requirements with numbers | Names requirements without numbers | Cannot say what the design had to meet |
| **Trade-off reasoning** (MS-ETS1-2, HS-ETS1-3) | Explains two trade-offs with costs and benefits on both sides | Explains one trade-off clearly | Describes a choice but not what it cost | Choices made at random |
| **Use of evidence from the simulation** (MS-ETS1-4, HS-ETS1-4) | Uses results numbers ("what shaped this mission") to justify a change, and explains the gap to the prediction | Refers to a result in general terms | Reports the score only | No reference to results |
| **Orbits and windows** (HS-ESS1-4, HS-PS2-4) | Explains the 26-month window and the transfer time with the class's own dates | Explains the window in general terms | Knows windows exist, not why | No connection |
| **Real-world connection** (HS-ETS1-1) | Links a real NASA lesson to a process fix and to their own project | Retells a real NASA lesson accurately | Mentions a story with errors | No connection |
| **Communication** | Clear, concise, uses the right terms (reserve, margin, review, window) | Clear with minor gaps | Partly unclear | Unclear |
